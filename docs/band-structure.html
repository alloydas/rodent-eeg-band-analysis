Five bands, two numbers

Delta, theta, alpha, beta and gamma are contiguous slices of one spectrum, so asking whether they are related is really asking how many independent measurements you actually have. The answer is about two — and the two that matter are the spectrum’s overall level and its slope.

Every number here comes from the lab’s epoch export, filtered to channel_index = 1 and features_valid = 1, with RoomD/RN245 dropped as instructed. Correlations are computed inside each animal and then summarised across the 23, because between-animal amplitude spans roughly 4× with no calibration signal — pooling first would manufacture agreement that is not there.

01 — How much information

The bands correlate strongly, and nearly all of it is one shared factor

Within an animal, every band pair is positively correlated, from 0.89 for alpha–beta down to 0.26 for delta–gamma. The gradient is orderly: the closer two bands sit in frequency, the harder they move together.

A single principal component absorbs a median 71.4% of the variance, and the participation ratio — the honest count of how many dimensions are really in play — is 1.83. Five columns, two numbers’ worth of information.

Conditioning changes the picture sharply. The four neighbouring pairs stay strongly positive (delta–theta +0.52, theta–alpha +0.52, alpha–beta +0.60, beta–gamma +0.68, all 23/23 animals), while the distant pairs fall to zero or go negative: alpha–gamma −0.22, delta–beta −0.14, theta–gamma −0.09.

Two exceptions matter, because they are the only places the data departs from what geometry alone would predict. Theta–beta stays positive at +0.13 despite being two bands apart, in 22 of 23 animals and 23 of 23 recordings. And delta–beta goes negative where a smooth spectrum predicts positive. Those two sign reversals are the real conditional-structure result.

02 — Is it real

Not a pipeline artefact — but not five rhythms either

Contiguous bands integrated with the trapezoid rule share their edge bins, and a 4-second Hann window smears power across neighbours. Either could manufacture the adjacency gradient, so both were measured rather than assumed.

Neither does. Pushing signals with known independent band content through the exact export recipe induces a correlation of 0.05 at separation 1 and nothing beyond it. Under a realistic generator the recipe’s marginal contribution is +0.0010 ± 0.0004 — 0.13% of the observed 0.758. The shared edge bin adds +0.006. These mechanisms are dismissible.

So the relationship is real signal — but it is not evidence of five distinct oscillations. A generator with two free parameters per epoch, a level and a slope, and no coupling between bands at all, reproduces the entire gradient. One broadband factor accounts for roughly 98% of the off-diagonal correlation.

Where the data does beat the model. Residuals against the 1/f fit are not uniform. Theta–alpha (+0.14), alpha–beta (+0.15) and theta–beta (+0.12) are more coupled than a smooth spectrum predicts, while every gamma pairing is less: beta–gamma −0.23, alpha–gamma −0.25, theta–gamma −0.18.

Gamma is the outlier. It is the band least tied to the others, it barely moves across the day, and it tracks line length — an effective-frequency measure — far more closely than it tracks EEG amplitude. Treat it as carrying something the rest of the spectrum does not, and be cautious about reading it as cortical activity.

03 — Across the day

One light-locked switch, with frequency-graded gain

The bands do not each keep their own schedule. They are driven by a single transition tied to the room lights — a rapid fall around 17:50 and a roughly 90-minute rise beginning near 05:00 — and one component takes 97.8% of each animal’s 24-hour band structure.

But the gain on that switch is steeply graded by frequency. Alpha loads at 0.54 and swings 0.64–1.30 of its own daily median; gamma loads at 0.07 and barely moves. Line length runs the other way entirely, rising at night while total power falls.

Static correlation cannot see the second, smaller thing happening here. Inside the light block the spectrum slowly tilts: delta loses 0.23 percentage points of spectral share per hour (in 22 of 23 animals) while beta gains 0.10 and alpha 0.05 (both 23 of 23). Across the dark block it is flat. That drift is unanimous across the cohort and independent of the switch itself.

04 — During seizures

The bands shout “seizure” and whisper “how bad”

Seizures tilt the spectrum hard and consistently. Relative weight moves out of delta and theta and into beta and gamma — gamma rises 2.90 interictal standard deviations, delta barely moves at 0.10 and is not significant.

Severity is where it stops. Across Stage 2 to Stage 5 the gamma shift runs 1.06 / 1.04 / 1.27 / 1.25 — a total span of 0.23 z against an ictal-versus-interictal effect of 2.90. The bands detect the event; they do not grade it.

That shows up directly in discrimination. Every band, every log-ratio, and a 13-feature leave-one-animal-out model all land between 0.46 and 0.55 for severe-versus-mild once session is controlled for — while the same features reach 0.55–0.87 for simply detecting that a seizure is happening.

The pooled number is the trap. Score severity on session identity alone — how often that session’s seizures happen to be severe, with the EEG never consulted — and pooled AUROC reaches 0.927. Within session it collapses to exactly 0.5, because there is nothing in it. Any severity figure from this export that is not computed within session is measuring which session a clip came from.

What happens to the relationships themselves

Seizures also change how the bands relate. Four of ten pairwise correlations drop significantly after correction — theta–alpha −0.16, theta–beta −0.20, delta–alpha −0.24, alpha–beta −0.07 — and the share explained by one factor falls from 0.77 to 0.67.

That is easy to misread as desynchronisation, and it is not. In absolute terms both components grow: the shared broadband factor’s variance rises 1.42× (in 13 of 17 animals) and the band-specific residual rises 2.54× (in all 17). Seizures add differential energy faster than they add common energy, so the proportion attributable to one factor falls while synchrony itself rises.

05 — What to do with it

Practical consequences

  • Do not feed all five bands to a model as five features. Three bands — delta, alpha and gamma — predict the other two at R² 0.83 and 0.90. Two bands, delta and beta, recover a mean R² of 0.75.
  • Prefer level and slope. If you want two numbers that carry almost everything, use total power and a spectral tilt rather than a band subset. The 1/f model says so directly.
  • Never pool across animals before correlating. It raises mean r from 0.65 to 0.81 and would report 1.36 effective dimensions instead of 1.83, because the 4× amplitude span between animals is uncalibrated.
  • Never quote a pooled severity number. Session identity alone scores 0.927 pooled and 0.5 within session.
  • Use relative powers in log-ratio geometry. The five shares sum to exactly 1, so ordinary correlations between them are structurally negative and largely meaningless.
  • Treat gamma separately. It is the least coupled band, nearly flat across the day, and behaves like an effective-frequency measure rather than cortical power.

One caveat on the headline. The nearest-neighbour chain is not a discovered property of rodent EEG. Integrating any smooth log-spectrum over five contiguous bands produces it, with no physiology involved — a synthetic smooth-spectrum null reproduces the chain in full. What genuinely departs from that null is narrower: the positive theta–beta edge and the negative delta–beta edge, plus gamma’s systematic under-coupling.
